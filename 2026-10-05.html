Anthropic shipped "mods" for Claude Code, and twelve people wrote about Claude this week

Twelve different people wrote about Claude. It was not one story. It was three.

The mods story is the real news. Anthropic shipped mods on 3 October. A mod is a small add-on that sits inside Claude Code and can change how it behaves — rewrite a prompt, block a tool call, hide private details, or add things to the screen. Julian Goldie, Nate Herk and Jack Roberts all cover it, and they agree on the basics: you describe the mod you want in plain words and Claude Code writes it for you. Goldie has the hard details — you need version 2.1.287 or newer, mods are on by default, and a mod Claude builds for you only lasts one session unless you copy the folder out. Herk shows five he actually uses, including one that warns before the cache goes cold. Jack Roberts is the only one who raises the risk: mods can read and write files, start programs and use the network, and a third party found a mod that could read credential files and send them out. Roberts says to be careful what you download. Nobody else mentions security.

The Sonnet 5.5 story is separate. Anthropic released a mid-tier model that scores close to its top model at half the price. The rest is people using Claude for marketing work — Jono Catliff on local SEO, Ryan Doser arguing non-technical marketers should not be scared of the terminal, Duncan Rogoff listing GitHub agent repos, and Goldie selling an "Agent OS" through his paid community. Jack Roberts2 OctVideo
Claude + New Mods = Unlimited Possibilities

Jack Roberts says Claude Code's new mods are small add-ons that let people build custom tools around Claude Code, and they come with real security risks.

  • A mod is code that sits in the middle of a Claude Code event. It can watch the event, change it, or block it. It works only in the terminal or sidebar, not in the chat.
  • Mods can hide text, change a prompt or tool call, add new screen parts, add context, suggest next actions and route to other models. Examples shown: a playable Doom game, progress bars, a usage tracker and a context-window weather display.
  • Routing by query means a small model like Haiku can handle simple jobs. Jack Roberts says this stops people using "a bazooka" for small tasks. It needs an OpenRouter key.
  • Mods can read and write files, start programs, use the network and see settings files. A third party found a mod can read credential files and send them out. Jack Roberts says to be careful what you download.
  • Jack Roberts points to a free guide in the description. Claude uses it to interview you and help build mods for your needs.

Grow and Convert studied 15,000 AI answers and says your own site matters more than third-party mentions

Grow and Convert ran 112 prompts across ChatGPT, Gemini, AI Overviews, AI Mode and Perplexity. 85% of what the AI tools cited was third-party pages — but that is not what decided whether the client got mentioned. A new email reporting standard, APRF, is moving through the IETF

Matthew Vernhout explains APRF, a proposed standard that would have mailbox providers send senders a daily summary of how their authenticated email got sorted and how people engaged with it. Reports are totals only — never what one subscriber did. They cover classification (inbox, unwanted) and engagement (positive, neutral, negative), and each provider decides what counts as which. To receive them you publish a DNS record tied to your DKIM domain and selector, naming the email address for reports. Reports come as JSON files covering one UTC day. APRF started in March 2026, version -01 landed in September, and the IETF working group has issued a Call for Adoption — but it is still only a draft. Vernhout says early experimenting may suit email service providers, deliverability teams, technically mature senders, and anyone with a lot of Comcast readers. Support is limited, the spec may change, and reports are not guaranteed.

Only 7% of organisations have DMARC fully enforced, and that is the gate to a BIMI logo

Alison Gootee's recap of a Mailgun and Red Sift webinar says you cannot earn a BIMI logo in the inbox until SPF, DKIM and DMARC are set up properly. Only about 7% of organisations have reached DMARC enforcement — quarantine or reject at 100%. The other 93% sit at p=none, which only collects reports and blocks nothing. The route there: find every sender on your domain, read the reports for a few weeks, fix or remove bad records, then move to p=quarantine before p=reject. A CMC logo certificate costs about $1,000 a year for the first domain and about $450 for each extra domain on the same logo. Red Sift's own research claims a BIMI logo brought a 44% rise in brand recall, a 39% rise in open rates and a 32% rise in purchase intent — those are the vendor's numbers.
